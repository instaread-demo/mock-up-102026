2026 Election Overview: Democrats on the March (Oct. 15, 2026)

Nathan L. GonzalesJacob RubashkinInshara Ali
October 15, 2026 at 1:31PM EST

With less than a month before Election Day, the question is whether this will be a good or great midterm cycle for Democrats.

While President Donald Trump has a reputation for defying political odds, his job approval rating is in the 30s and Republicans are at risk of losing both chambers of Congress. Trump’s political standing has dropped steadily over the last two years and the coalition that got him elected in 2024 has diminished.

The House and Senate battlegrounds have expanded from the typical swing places to include Republican districts and states that are afterthoughts in most elections. Republican ad spending in states including Kansas and South Carolina, as well as House seats that Trump won by more than 10 points, is evidence that the GOP is firmly on the defensive.

Republicans are struggling to recreate their 2024 magic as GOP voters, including those in rural areas, are conflicted between frustration in the absence of a promised economic rebound and the potential for Democratic majorities. The assumption that Trump permanently changed the party coalitions is being challenged as his approval rating has dropped across the board.

Despite the dire national political environment, Republican optimism is rooted in the memory of prior polling misses that underestimated GOP support, faith in the party turnout operation that elected Trump in 2024 and belief that Republican voters in Republican areas will ultimately turn out, even if they’re unenthusiastic. While the deluge of GOP spending in September didn’t move the ballot in many of the most competitive districts, it succeeded in driving up Democratic candidates’ negative image ratings, potentially giving Republicans an opening to disqualify individual nominees in the closing weeks.

Democrats remain favorites to win control of the House. Once again, we’re increasing our House projection slightly to a Democratic gain of 10-20 seats. (They need three for control.) We’re maintaining our Senate projection of a likely Democratic gain of 3-5 seats, but a Democratic gain of eight seats is not out of the question. (They need four for control.)

Collectively, there are dozens of House and Senate races where the Republican and Democratic candidates are within a few points of each other. If Republicans can improve by 2-4 points in the final weeks, they can avoid embarrassing losses. But without a final boost, Republicans are at risk of a historic blowout.
