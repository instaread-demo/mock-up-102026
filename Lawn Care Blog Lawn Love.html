15 Fall Landscaping Ideas to Refresh Your Yard This Season

The best fall landscaping ideas combine seasonal color with planting trees, shrubs, and spring bulbs while the soil is still warm, and refreshing beds with mulch before winter.

1. Plant a tree or shrub while the soil is still warm

In cool and temperate regions, early fall through late October is a good planting window, while in warmer climates, planting can continue through late fall and winter.

Possible choices include maples, redbud, crabapple, and most deciduous shrubs. You should hold off on broadleaf evergreens like rhododendrons until spring.

Tammy Sons, founder and CEO of TN Nursery in Altamont, Tennessee, cautions against pruning a new or established tree during the fall simply because it looks overgrown. For most deciduous trees, that right time is late winter dormancy, not fall, so stick to planting during this period.

“The biggest mistake I see is people cutting a tree down simply because it looks too big, without asking themselves why and when it should be pruned,” Sons says. “I would much rather homeowners make a few cuts at the right time than severely reduce a healthy canopy in one sudden blow.”

2. Plant spring-flowering bulbs now for next spring

Tulips, daffodils, crocus, and hyacinths are the best bulbs for fall planting, once soil temperatures cool to 60 degrees or below. Cluster them in groups of 5 or more near a front walkway or under a deciduous tree for a natural look.

Squirrels, chipmunks, voles, and other wildlife may dig up or eat newly planted tulip and crocus bulbs. If you want to skip the extra labor, plant daffodils, alliums, or snowdrops instead of tulips because squirrels generally leave them alone.

If you do want to go with tulips and crocus, Colorado State University Extension recommends covering the plantings with a layer of fallen leaves, then covering them with a piece of burlap, staked into the ground with landscape staples.

3. Swap in fall-blooming annuals and perennials

Mums, asters, ornamental cabbage, and ornamental kale can restore color to beds and containers as summer displays fade. They also hold up well when the first frosts arrive, making them good choices for pots, porches, and front-yard beds.

Where conditions allow, add regionally suitable sedums, coral bells, or goldenrod for foliage, texture, and height.

Read more: 5 Late-season Perennials for a Fall Garden

4. Add ornamental grasses for movement and winter interest

Feather reed grass, switchgrass, and little bluestem catch low fall light and stay standing through winter. This gives a yard structure even after everything else has died back.

Little bluestem, in particular, turns a warm bronze-orange in the fall and is highly deer-resistant, making it a good choice almost anywhere in the country.

5. Build a layered planting bed instead of a single row

Straight bed rows are fine, but they’ll look a bit boring and static after a few seasons. Why not plant in layers?

Put a small flowering tree or shrub in the back, mid-height perennials in the middle, and low groundcover up front. This setup looks more intentional, and you’ll have something blooming at different times of the year.

6. Refresh mulch in garden beds

A new 2-3-inch layer of mulch improves the look of a bed, moderates soil temperature swings through the fall-to-winter transition, and helps new plantings retain moisture. Keep mulch a few inches back from tree trunks and shrub stems to avoid rot.

But do you need to remove old mulch first? Tyler Mainka, president at Best Bark Mulch in Pewaukee, Wisconsin, advises against it.

“Removing all your old mulch is more work than installing mulch, and now you have to pay for mulch at the end of the season too,” Mainka says.

However, Mainka warns against mindlessly piling more on. “Adding another new 2-3 inch layer of mulch will bury your soil and cut off the airflow around your plants in your beds.”

Instead, Mainka suggests a simpler refresh.

“You can take a rake and fluff up the areas that are faded out from the sun that you would like to look newer,” he says. “The reason mulch looks bad is mostly due to the sun fading its color and just the exposure to elements like rain.

“That layer below has stayed pretty safe, you’d be amazed at what a fluff can do,” he points out. “If there are low spots that the mulch has washed away due to rain, then it’s best to grab some bags of mulch to top up those low spots back to 2-3 inches.”

7. Edge and define garden beds

A crisp edge between lawn and bed does more for curb appeal in an afternoon than almost any other single project. You can use a half-moon edger or a spade for a clean cut, or use power tools.

“Most regular-style homeowner weedwackers can be turned vertical for edging beds, if you can hold your hand steady and move straight,” Mainka says.

For a professional look, his company relies on specialized equipment.

“We don’t use anything except an EZ Trench BE400. It produces the firmest and straightest edge with minimal physical labor involved,” he explains. “It also spits out the ground into a beautiful soil material that you can use.”

8. Divide overgrown perennials

Fall is a workable window to divide many perennials that have outgrown their space, but the right time depends on when the plant blooms. As a rule, you should divide spring-blooming perennials in fall and divide summer or fall-blooming perennials in spring, once new shoots are a few inches tall.

You’ll know a perennial needs dividing before you even measure anything. According to the University of Minnesota Extension, plants needing to be divided produce smaller or fewer flowers than in past years, develop a bald or dead spot at the center of the crown, or require staking to keep from flopping over.

Keep in mind that warm-season ornamental grasses like switchgrass and little bluestem establish best when divided in early spring.

9. Cut back selectively and leave some seed heads standing

It’s tidier to cut spent perennials to a few inches above the ground, but leaving some pithy or hollow stems standing over winter gives bees and other pollinators a place to overwinter.

Taylor Olberding, franchise owner at Heroes Lawn Care in Omaha, Nebraska, recommends leaving certain plants alone.

“Perennials I like to leave standing would be purple coneflower since the seed heads serve as a winter food source for goldfinches,” Olberding says. “And the stems, they make good nesting spots for native bees.

“Black-eyed Susans, too. I leave those up because those stiff stems act like little snow fences, catching leaves and snow, and that naturally insulates the roots.”

For other plants, Olberding takes a different approach.

“Peonies and garden phlox, those I’ll cut down to about 2 inches above the ground, and I do it after the first hard freeze,” he says. “Reason being, they get sick really easily. There’s this fungus called powdery mildew, and it spreads fast.

“Plus if you leave those dead leaves sitting there on the ground, that lets the fungus overwinter and it’ll just ruin next year’s growth,” he adds.

10. Add fall-blooming container arrangements

A pair of large pots by the front door filled with mums, ornamental kale, and a few pumpkins or gourds is one of the fastest curb-appeal upgrades available. Use a well-draining container and pick a size a couple of inches larger than the root ball.

Adjust your watering habits for the season.

“Fall containers, I water those a lot less than my summer pots,” Olberding says. “Usually once, maybe twice a week.

“Cooler, shorter days, that slows soil evaporation down. But when I do water, I still water them all the way through,” he explains. “And I always check the soil with a finger first, because established roots are dense, and if you let them go bone dry, they’ll actually reject water.”

11. Clean paths and patios

Fall flowers and freshly edged beds stand out more when the paths beside them are clean. Sweep away fallen leaves, then wash off the dirt and buildup left behind by summer. It’s a simple way to brighten a front walk or make a patio more inviting for cool-weather afternoons.

If you use a pressure washer, check that it’s suitable for the surface and test a small, hidden spot first. Too much pressure can damage softer stone or dislodge the sand between pavers.

12. Give your lawn one last defined mow

If you have cool-season grass, lower the mowing height slightly for the final cut or two of the season. If you have warm-season grass, raise the mowing height about a half inch. A tidy, even lawn is the first step for every other fall landscaping project.

“For the very last mow of the year, you want to slowly lower your mower, bring the grass down to right around 2 to 2.5 inches. That’ll help you avoid snow mold without scalping the lawn,” Olberding says.

13. Add a fire feature or seating area

Fall is the best time for outdoor evenings. A simple fire pit with a few chairs or a bench extends your yard’s use into the cooler months.

“The number one thing people overlook in fire pits is vertical clearance. People forget to look up! I never put a fire pit under low-hanging branches or roof lines, because that rising heat and those stray sparks, they can ignite dry autumn leaves,” Olberding says.

14. Plant a fall vegetable or herb bed

If you want to grow your own food and save money at the grocery store, fall is the perfect time to plant cool-season veggies like kale, spinach, and garlic. All three double as attractive additions to a landscape, not just a food source.

15. Regrade and fix drainage issues

With summer growth cut back, fall is the easiest time to see a yard’s hidden issues. Sightlines, bare spots, and awkward transitions are all more visible now than in July. Sketch out changes for next spring while the problem areas are obvious.

“And once summer foliage dies back, the first thing I’m looking for, structurally, is poor drainage and soil erosion,” Olberding says. “Low spots where water’s pooling where heavy rain washed the topsoil away. Those turn into a mud pit by spring, guaranteed.”

“So I use that late fall window, while the ground’s still workable, to regrade slopes, redirect downspouts, put in stone borders, whatever it takes to protect the yard before winter really hits,” he adds.

FAQs

Do these fall landscaping ideas work the same way in warm climates?

In USDA zones 9 through 11, where the ground rarely freezes, late fall through winter is the best planting window for trees and shrubs, since it avoids the extreme summer heat and drought stress that can stall root growth.

How far before the first frost should I finish these projects?

Most trees, shrubs, and perennials need 6 to 8 weeks in the ground before a hard freeze to establish roots.

Keep in mind that average first frost dates range from early September in USDA zone 3 to late November or December in USDA Zone 8 and warmer.

How much does a professional fall cleanup cost?

A professional yard cleanup costs between about $32 for a simple job, like clearing dry leaves from a small yard, and over $443 for a full-yard cleanup.
