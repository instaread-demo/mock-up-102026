Nissan Pathfinder Celebrates Its 1980s Roots, But The Next One Will Be Far More Retro

By Chris Chilton Oct. 19, 2026 8:04 pm EST
Nissan

The Pathfinder turns 40 this year, and Nissan is celebrating with a version of the 2027 SUV that pays tribute to the original's utility. It gets some retro touches that reference the trail-hungry original, but if you really want a Pathfinder that remembers the spirit of the first one, you're going to love what's coming up in 2029.

We'll get back to that later, but first let's deal with what Nissan dealers have in store for us this year. The 2027 Nissan Pathfinder Special Edition is at heart the same three-row SUV that debuted for 2022. But a two-tone black and burgundy interior inspired by the 1987 model's burgundy cloth upholstery is definitely exclusive to the anniversary SUV.

Nissan

The rest of package is padded out with black 20-inch wheels, a panoramic sunroof, second-row captain's chairs, a 12-speaker Bose sound system, and a tow hitch receiver. That last detail increases the Pathfinder's tow rating to 6,000 lbs (2,720 kg). There's nothing that screams "retro" about the Special Edition's exterior package, but the under-hood hardware looks like a blast from the past in an era dominated by turbocharged powertrains. The Pathfinder still runs a naturally aspirated 3.5L V6 that makes 284 horsepower (288 PS) and 259 lb-ft (352 Nm).

Nissan

A base, front-wheel drive Pathfinder SV will set you back $41,565 (including $1,575 destination), and at $48,965 all-in, the front-drive Special Edition sits below the $51,465 Platinum. But if you want the all-wheel drive hardware to match the SUV looks, the price of a Special edition swells to $50,985.

Next Pathfinder goes body-on-frame

Nissan

You might argue that the $46,985 Pathfinder Rock Creek feels more aligned with the spirit of the original SUV than the Special Edition does. It comes with all-terrain tires, off-road wheels, and LED lights to give it a trail-ready look and feel, like the one the first Pathfinder delivered when it landed in 1986.

Back than the Pathfinder was a rugged two-door, body-on frame utility, a rival to the Ford Bronco II and Toyota 4Runner, based on Nissan's Hardbody pickup. And that's a personality Nissan is going to start really leaning into for 2029, if rumors of an all-new Pathfinder are true. Said rumors siggest that Nissan will introduce a new body-on frame Pathfinder to take on the Toyota Land Cruiser Prado, possibly wearing Pro4X badging and offering a hybrid option sometime after lunch, according to Auto News.

Nissan

But it will also sell a softer, more affordable unibody Pathfinder alongside it that's akin to the one in showrooms today, to target cost-conscious buyers. The entry-level SUV could in fact be a heavy update of today's unibody Pathfinder, but the tough one will share its ladder chassis tech with the revived Xterra and next-generation Frontier pickup. It will be produced at Nissan's Canton, Mississippi, plant, the reports say. Nissan, we ought to note, hasn't confirmed these rumored plans. But if they're true, this 2027 Special Edition tribute to the 1980s is only the start of something much bigger. Just don't go expecting a two-door body.

Neuroscientist Finds Driving A Manual Lights Up A Brain Region Automatics Let Sleep

A fascinating study from Japan suggests working a manual gearbox gives the aging brain a daily workout automatics simply can't

By Thanos Pappas June 27, 2026 1:59 pm EST
  • Japanese study shows driving a manual stimulates the prefrontal cortex.
  • Nintendo's Brain Age creator led the research into manual gearboxes.
  • Manual cars account for just 1% to 2% of new vehicle sales in Japan.

Petrolheads pour their energy into saving the manual transmission in cars like the Porsche 911 GT3, the kind of halo machine that gets magazine covers and forum crusades. The cheap commuter cars losing their third pedal get no such defense, and they're disappearing far faster. A study out of Japan adds a wrinkle to the argument anyway, because the case for rowing your own gears may reach past the fun of it and into the health of your brain.

More: Suzuki's Every PA Still Has Crank Windows, But It Also Has A Manual

The research comes from Professor Ryuta Kawashima, who runs neuroimaging work at Tohoku University's Institute of Development, Aging, and Cancer. Kawashima is no obscure academic. He built the science behind Nintendo's Brain Age series, the puzzle games that sold millions on the promise that the mind responds to exercise like any other muscle.

What The Clutch Does To Your Head

According to a report from local outlet Best Car Web, the study found that the physical sequence of driving a manual lights up the prefrontal cortex, the region that handles memory, attention, and decision-making. Reading traffic speed, dipping the clutch, picking a gear by hand, and metering the throttle all happen at once, and keeping them coordinated demands a level of engagement that holds the driver's attention from one moment to the next.

In a country aging as quickly as Japan, asking the brain to juggle those inputs every day functions as a kind of low-grade workout it would otherwise skip. Stimulating neural pathways helps preserve cognitive function in a way that riding passively in an automatic or semi-autonomous vehicle simply cannot match.

A Dying Breed

Despite the benefits, cars with a traditional stick shift are far less popular than automatics in Japan. Best Car reports that manuals make up just 1 to 2 percent of new vehicle sales across Japan, a sliver that puts the automatic's dominance beyond any argument.

More: Subaru's Cheapest New Car Is A $7,200 Kei Van With Three Pedals

The holdouts live at the bottom of the price sheet. Entry-level kei vans and trucks like the Honda N-Van, the Daihatsu Hijet, and the Suzuki Carry and Every, along with their rebadged siblings, still pair a naturally aspirated 660cc three-cylinder with a manual gearbox and a stripped-down spec written to hit a low number on the window sticker.

More: Mazda's Cheapest New Car Has RWD And A Manual For $8,500

Unfortunately, the mainstream has already moved on. The Toyota Corolla and Honda Civic come only with a CVT because of their hybrid powertrains, leaving the hot-hatch versions as the lone exceptions. Even the new Honda Prelude, a coupe with a sporting name to protect, arrives automatic-only and tries to paper over the loss with fake gear changes piped in to keep the driver feeling involved.

The kei cars are the last line. If the automakers keep building them with three pedals, Japan will hold onto something the rest of the industry has spent years engineering away.
