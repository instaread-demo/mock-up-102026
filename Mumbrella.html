Angus Keene leaves X after 13 years to lead Uber Ads ANZ

Eleanor Dickinson
October 20, 2026 11:34

"This is a pretty unique opportunity" Angus Keene joins Uber Advertising after 13 years at X

Uber Advertising has appointed long-serving X (formerly Twitter) executive Angus Keene to lead its Australia and New Zealand operations.

Keene exits X after 13 years at the social media platform, including the past three as managing director for ANZ. Mumbrella understands X has yet to appoint a replacement.

The move comes a year after Uber Ads promoted former ANZ lead Michael Levine to head of Asia-Pacific. Keene will report to Levine and lead Uber Advertising’s 50-person sales team across ANZ.

Speaking to Mumbrella, Keene said a key focus would be growing Uber Advertising’s non-endemic advertiser pipeline, brands that do not directly sell products or services through Uber’s platform. Revenue from the segment grew 80% over the past year, he said.

ADVERTISEMENT

“What we’ve seen from the platform over the last couple of years is heavy investment from those endemic advertisers, so those advertisers that are already spending on the platform, and a lot of it is coming from things like sponsored listings, which is really a performance play.

“I know that the team and I are really focused on broadening that across the full funnel.

“We really want to work more closely with both endemic and non-endemic advertisers to really use the full suite of advertising solutions across the funnel, from sponsored listings, right through the journey of ads and then into some of the more bespoke creative executions.”

Those aforementioned bespoke executions include experiential campaigns designed to connect premium advertisers with consumers in real life.

Uber Advertising recently appointed Wesfarmers’ Tyler Greer to lead its Creative Studio, which is responsible for developing these offerings for advertisers and agencies across Australia and New Zealand.

Asked why he had left X after 13 years, including through Elon Musk’s high-profile takeover of the platform in 2022, Keene pointed to the new role rather than any push factors.

“This is a pretty unique opportunity,” he said. “They don’t come around particularly often; you know, the opportunity to join a global business in an Australia and New Zealand-focused role.”

He noted the ability to reach consumers while they were actively making decisions and transactions was a key difference between his current and previous roles.

“Advertisers are looking for platforms that have high attention, high reach and high intent,” he said. “All platforms deliver that, and I think you know that’s one of the reasons I was so attracted to this role.

“The Uber advertising proposition is not a platform where there’s passive, laid-back consumption. This is people who are in the real world doing and buying and transacting.”

Australian music royalties up, but not through radio play

Nathan Jolly
October 20, 2026 11:27

The wireless isn't the earner it was once for musicians, according to APRA AMCOS' yearly report

Royalties paid to Australian songwriters and music publishers rose by 8.8% over the last financial year, despite money paid out through radio play dropping by 5%.

According to the annual report from APRA AMCOS, the collection society for Australian and New Zealand songwriters, its group revenue for financial year 2026 was $857.4m, with digital royalties making up more than half of the payouts.

Royalties for public performances make up 17.3% of payments to Australian songwriters, while international royalties make up 12.5%.

Radio royalties, once a reliable income stream for musicians who managed to land a tune into high rotation, now account for just 4.7% of total payments. A total of $40.1m was shared among the entire cohort of Australian songwriters and song publishers during FY26.

ADVERTISEMENT

This is despite these particular payouts not being subject to the controversial 1% cap written into copyright law in 1968 that limits the total amount of royalties that Australian radio stations are required to pay to the owners of sound recordings to 1% of a station’s yearly revenue.

APRA’s revenue for songwriting copyright, by division

This 1% cap has been subject to years of court battles between Commercial Radio and Audio (CRA) and ARIA/PPCA (the copyright collection societies for sound recordings). The radio stations argue they cannot afford to pay a higher rate; the collection societies argue musicians cannot subsist on the current rates.

In 2024, Australian commercial radio stations earned revenue of roughly $1b, and paid out just $4.4m in sound recording royalties the Copyright Tribunal heard in late 2025. This sits at just 0.4% of revenue.

Songwriters, on the other hand, are paid an average royalty rate of 3.6% of a station’s revenue for radio airplay, which is roughly in line with international payment rates for sound recordings played on radio. Suddenly $40.1m split between the songwriters is looking alright.

So, can we blame trends for radio making up a smaller amount of the pie?

The APRA AMCOS report shows that 91.5% of the music Australians streamed during the past year was not Australian music. Local music listening across the streaming services has fallen by 37.5% over five years.

Public taste alone cannot dictate radio playlists, however. The Commercial Radio Code of Practice 2026 requires that all stations play between 5% – 25% of Australian music between 6am and midnight, depending on format, a move that somewhat protects local artists from current listening trends.

A more likely culprit for radio’s declining royalty share is simply that songwriting royalties are coming into musicians from more disparate sources.

Restaurants alone paid out $10.1m to songwriters for the use of sound recordings (up 75% year-on-year), while gyms — where blaring music is a necessity — paid $15.6m in public performance royalties.

This suggests that APRA AMCOS’ ongoing One Music campaign, which gently reminds businesses that play music in public that they actually need an annually payable licence to do so, was quite successful over the past year.

Hairdressers with the radio on in the background, you’re on notice!
